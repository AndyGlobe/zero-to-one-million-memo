Where Does the Return Assumption Come From?

The return percentage used in this tool is an input for scenario analysis — a placeholder that lets you explore how different growth rates affect the time needed to reach a target. It is not a prediction of what any investment will actually earn.

Actual investment returns depend on many factors, including:

  • Asset mix — the proportion of stocks, bonds, cash, and other holdings.
  • Market performance — which varies significantly from year to year and is inherently unpredictable.
  • Fees and expenses — fund management fees, transaction costs, and advisory fees reduce net returns.
  • Taxes — investment gains may be taxed, reducing the amount available to compound.
  • Inflation — erodes purchasing power, so a nominal return may represent a much smaller real return.
  • Volatility — the sequence of good and bad years can materially affect outcomes, especially over shorter periods.

Historical averages for broad market indices are sometimes used as rough reference points, but past performance does not guarantee future results. A higher assumed return produces a shorter projected timeline, but it also implies greater risk and uncertainty. There is no return rate that any investor can reliably count on.

Cash / Deposits

Lower expected return / Lower volatility

Role: Capital preservation and liquidity. Often used for emergency funds or short-term needs.

Relative risk: Low — value is stable, but purchasing power may erode due to inflation.

Return expectation: Generally lower. Interest rates on deposits tend to be modest.

Caveat: Inflation can cause the real (inflation-adjusted) return on cash to be negative over long periods.

Bonds

Moderate expected return / Moderate volatility

Role: Income generation and portfolio stability. Typically less volatile than equities.

Relative risk: Low to moderate — subject to interest rate risk and credit/default risk.

Return expectation: Generally moderate. Historically between cash and equities over long periods.

Caveat: Rising interest rates cause existing bond prices to fall; not all bonds are equally safe.

Mixed Portfolio

Moderate expected return / Moderate volatility

Role: Balanced growth and stability. Combines equities and bonds in varying proportions.

Relative risk: Moderate — diversification reduces but does not eliminate losses.

Return expectation: Moderate. Blends the higher potential of equities with the relative stability of bonds.

Caveat: The specific mix matters greatly — a 90/10 stock/bond allocation behaves very differently from 50/50.

Broad Equities

Higher expected return / Higher volatility

Role: Long-term growth. Represents ownership in a diversified basket of companies.

Relative risk: Higher — prices can decline significantly in the short to medium term.

Return expectation: Generally higher over long periods, but with substantial year-to-year variation.

Caveat: Higher historical average returns come with the risk of prolonged drawdowns and significant short-term losses.

The return assumption in the calculator is a scenario input, not a forecast. Different asset classes have historically produced different return patterns, but future returns are uncertain.

How Compounding Works

Compounding means you earn returns not just on your original money, but also on the returns that money has already generated. In year one, your $500 earns a little interest. In year two, you earn interest on the original $500 plus that first year's interest — and on every monthly contribution along the way.

Early on, progress feels slow because the balance is small and contributions do most of the work. But as the balance grows, the returns themselves become large enough to add meaningful money each month — and the larger balance generates even larger returns. This self-reinforcing cycle is why the curve bends upward over time, and why time in the market matters more than timing the market.

The scenarios above show the power of the return rate: a 6-percentage-point difference between the 4% and 10% scenarios shortens the journey by roughly three decades.
